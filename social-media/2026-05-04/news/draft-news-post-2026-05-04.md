# How to Keep Your Sourdough Fresh All Week (It's Simpler Than You Think)

*Store it right, and your Saturday loaf is still incredible on Thursday.*

You picked up a beautiful loaf at the Castell Street Market on Saturday morning. By Sunday it's still perfect. Monday? Still good. Then Tuesday rolls around and somehow it tastes like it aged five years overnight. Sound familiar?

Here's the thing: that's almost never the bread's fault. It's storage. And once you know the rules, your sourdough will hold beautifully for 4–5 days — sometimes longer.

## Why Sourdough Stays Fresh Longer (The Short Version)

Sourdough isn't just shaped differently than commercial bread — it's *chemically* different. The long fermentation that gives sourdough its tang also produces **lactic and acetic acids**, which act as natural preservatives. Those same acids lower the bread's pH, making it a hostile environment for mold.

Compare that to a grocery store loaf, which might last a week in the bag because of added preservatives, calcium propionate, and other things you'd need a chemistry degree to pronounce. Our sourdough has none of that — and yet with the right storage, it keeps just as long, with a crust that actually behaves.

The catch? Sourdough needs to breathe. And plastic doesn't let it breathe.

## The #1 Rule: Keep It Cut-Side Down, No Bag

Once you slice a loaf, the exposed crumb is vulnerable. The best move is to **place the loaf cut-side down on a wooden cutting board** and leave it there at room temperature. The board and the crust act as a natural seal.

No bag. No plastic wrap. No airtight container. Just the loaf, cut-side down, on your counter.

This sounds wrong — like the bread will dry out. But a proper sourdough crust is built to protect the crumb. It will firm up on the outside while the interior stays soft. That's the crust doing its job.

## If You Need to Store It Longer

For 4–5 day storage, a **paper bag or a clean cloth bread bag** works well. Wrap the loaf (whole or partial) loosely — you want airflow, not a sealed environment.

Avoid the refrigerator. Cold temps accelerate starch retrogradation, which is the technical way of saying: your bread will go stale faster in the fridge than on the counter. It'll feel dry and dense in ways that have nothing to do with age.

## The Freezer Is Your Best Friend

If you know you won't finish the loaf within five days, **slice it before freezing**. Individual slices toast directly from frozen — straight from the freezer to the toaster, no thawing needed — and they taste genuinely great. This is how we recommend most people handle a full loaf if they live alone or with one other person.

For whole loaves, wrap tightly in plastic, then foil, and freeze for up to three months. Thaw at room temperature overnight.

## A Note on Our Different Loaves

Each variety has slightly different storage behavior because of its ingredients:

- **Jalapeño Cheddar** and **Italian Parmesan** — the fat in the cheese keeps the crumb moist a little longer than our plain loaf.
- **Lemon Blueberry** and **Orange Cranberry Walnut** — fruit moisture means these are best in the first 2–3 days at room temp, then freeze the rest.
- **Cinnamon Brown Sugar** — same as fruit loaves; eat fresh or freeze early.
- **Whole Wheat** — slightly drier crumb, benefits most from the cloth bag method.

## Reviving a Loaf That's Gone a Bit Stale

Even a 4-day-old loaf can be brought back. Run the cut side briefly under water (yes, water — just a quick pass), then place the whole loaf in a 350°F oven for 8–10 minutes. The steam reactivates the crumb. It won't taste like it just came out of our oven, but it'll be close. Add butter and it's hard to tell the difference.
